Two zodiacs are in use in the world, and they no longer line up. The gap between them is the ayanāṁśa. Everything on this page is computed in your browser while you read it — there is no number here that was typed into the prose — and the whole argument can be followed without any mathematics at all. The equations are here too, drawn rather than stated, for anyone who wants them.

Start here: two different zerosद्वौ आरम्भौ

A zodiac is a circle divided into twelve. To divide a circle you must first say where it starts. There are two reasonable answers, and the whole subject follows from the fact that they are different answers.

Start at the seasons

Put zero where the Sun stands at the spring equinox — the moment day and night are equal. This is the tropical zodiac. Its zero is tied to the Earth’s seasons, so the first degree of its first sign always means “spring begins”.

Start at the stars

Put zero at a fixed point among the stars. This is the sidereal zodiac, and it is the one Jyotiṣa uses. Its zero is tied to the actual constellations, so a graha said to be in Meṣa is somewhere near the stars of Meṣa.

If the Earth never wobbled, those two zeros would be the same point forever and nobody would ever have needed the word. The Earth does wobble. The ayanāṁśa is simply how far apart the two zeros have drifted — one number, in degrees, that converts a position measured from the equinox into a position measured from the stars.

In one line. Sidereal longitude = tropical longitude − ayanāṁśa. That subtraction is the entire job. Everything else on this page is about which number to subtract, and what happens when you change it.

Watch the gap openअन्तरं वर्धते

Drag the year. The outer ring is the sky — fixed to the stars, so it never turns. The inner ring is the seasons, which creep backwards against the stars by about one degree every seventy-two years. The shaded wedge between the two zero marks is the ayanāṁśa at that year.

Why the equinox moves at allअयन-चलनम्

Spin a top and it does not simply stand up: its axis leans, and that lean slowly circles around. The Earth does exactly this. It is tilted about 23½°, and the Sun and Moon pull on its equatorial bulge, so the axis swings round a cone and takes roughly 25,800 years to come back to where it began.

The equinox is defined by where the Earth’s equator cuts the Sun’s path. Move the axis and you move the equator with it, so the equinox point slides backwards along the zodiac — about 50 arcseconds a year, which is one degree in roughly seventy-two years and a whole sign in about 2,150. The stars stay where they are. The seasons do not. The ayanāṁśa is the running total of that slide.

Nothing is going wrong. This is not an error in anybody’s astronomy and not a correction for one. It is a real motion of the Earth, known to Hipparchus in the second century BCE and discussed in the siddhāntas as ayana-calana. Both zodiacs are internally consistent; they are answers to two different questions.

    The middle term does nearly all the work: it is the one degree per seventy-two years. The last term is tiny over any human span and matters only when you reach centuries away — which is exactly why different definitions agree about rate and disagree about starting value.

    The other kind of definition. A few ayanāṁśas are not a formula at all. They name a star and insist it sit at a stated longitude — Citrā (Spica) at exactly 180°, or Revatí (ζ Piscium) at 359°50′. The ayanāṁśa is then whatever number makes that true today, recomputed from the star’s real position. These move slightly differently from any polynomial, because a star has a motion of its own.

    The ones pinned to a starयोगतारा-बद्ध

    Not every definition is a formula. Some name a junction star — the yogatārā a nakṣatra is named for — and declare that it shall sit at a stated longitude. The ayanāṁśa is then whatever number makes that true. Three of the twenty-seven are used this way.

    Held, or merely set down once

    Several definitions can name the same star and still disagree, and the plot below shows by how much. A held definition recomputes the ayanāṁśa from the star for every date, so the star stays exactly at its stated longitude — that is the flat line at zero. The others either set the star down at one epoch and then advanced the whole frame by precession, or fitted a polynomial to the same anchor. Each is drawn as its distance from the held one, in arcminutes.

    Two different things are visible, and they are worth telling apart. Some of these curves are nearly flat and far from zero — a standing disagreement about where the star should be put, inherited from a different epoch or a different measurement of the star, which barely changes over two thousand years. Others start near zero and walk away, and only that second kind is drift in the sense of a frame slowly losing the star it was named for. The figures under the plot give both numbers for each definition: how far away it is now, and how much that distance moved across the span shown.

    Neither is a mistake. Holding a star keeps the frame tied to the sky you can actually see, at the cost of inheriting that one star’s own motion and the error in measuring it. Setting it down once keeps the frame smooth and predictable, at the cost of slowly parting from the very star it was named for. The plot is that trade-off measured rather than argued — and it was measured before this paragraph was written, which is why it does not claim that every curve drifts. Most of the Citrā family’s disagreement turns out to be a standing offset, not a drift at all.

    What it changes — and what it cannotफल-भेदः

    This is the part most worth knowing, and it is not what most people expect. Changing the ayanāṁśa does not change the sky. Every graha is exactly where it was; only the label you give its position changes. So some things move, and some things are mathematically incapable of moving.

    Why tithi cannot move. A tithi is the gap between the Moon and the Sun. Subtract the same ayanāṁśa from both and the gap is unchanged — it cancels, exactly, for every definition that exists or could ever be invented. The same goes for karaṇa, which is half a tithi, and for vāra, which is just the day of the week. A yoga, however, is the sum of the two, so the ayanāṁśa does not cancel there — it doubles. Change the ayanāṁśa by one degree and the yoga quantity moves by two. The figures above are measured, so you can watch that happen.

    Nineteen answers to one questionमत-भेदः

    This site offers nineteen definitions. They disagree with each other by more than five degrees — and the engine’s own uncertainty about where a graha is sits near one arcsecond. The choice of convention matters about a thousand times more than the quality of the arithmetic. If this site and another disagree about a chart, this is almost always why.

    Which of them the evidence actually allowsप्रमाण-सीमा

    Nineteen definitions exist, but they are not nineteen equally supported answers. Two of the sidereal zero’s constraints can be checked against the sky independently of each other — the twenty-seven yogatārā dhruvakās recorded in the Sūrya Siddhānta, and the solar apogee as three separate texts state it — and combined they place the zero at 493.6 ± 23 CE. That is a band, not a point, and the definitions that fall inside it are listed below.

    Two different mistakes are worth avoiding here. The first is treating the band as a ranking, and picking the convention nearest its centre: the conventions inside it agree with one another to a fraction of an arcsecond, so there is nothing to pick between, and the panel below measures that rather than asserting it. The second is forgetting that the band has a width. An uncertainty of ±23 years of epoch is about twenty arcminutes of ayanāṁśa, and twenty arcminutes is not nothing — it is what the rest of this section is about.
    This is a band on a historical question, not a verdict on which ayanāṁśa to read charts with. What is measured above is fidelity to the Sūrya Siddhānta’s own recorded star positions and solar apogee — where the fifth-century sky was, by the text’s own numbers. That is the right test for a convention claiming to reconstruct the siddhāntic zero. It is not the test for Lahiri, which is a stipulation — Citrā held at 180° — and is the civil standard the published Indian pañcāṁgas use. Against that standard Lahiri is correct by definition and no measurement can touch it. That is why Lahiri stays one choice away on every page, and on each almanac page by itself, even though the site’s charts now default to Samanvaya–Ecliptic. The gap between them is not academic: it is close to three degrees, or nine-tenths of a pāda. The two questions have different right answers, and the whole point of drawing the band is to keep them apart.
