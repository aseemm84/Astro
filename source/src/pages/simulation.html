Every trajectory on this page is integrated, not drawn. Nothing here scripts an outcome: a scenario changes a position, a velocity or a mass, and what follows is whatever summing the forces produces.

What this is

Ask what would happen if the Moon moved an inch away, or stopped revolving, and there are two ways to answer. One is to decide what would happen and then draw it. The other is to take the real Earth, the real Moon and the real Sun — their actual positions and velocities for the moment you choose, from this site's own ephemeris — change the one thing, and integrate the equations of motion to find out.

Only the second can surprise you, and being able to be surprised is the whole point. So when the Moon falls into the Earth in four and a half days, that is where the integrator put it, and you can check the figure against the free-fall time you can work out with a pencil.

The simulator knows its own resolution. Asked about an inch, it will tell you that the change is far below anything it or any simulation could show — and then give you the arithmetic instead. A confident little animation of a fifth of a millisecond would be a lie, and refusing to draw it is the more useful answer.

Choose a counterfactual

Could there still be an eclipse?

Whether a total solar eclipse is possible at all comes down to one comparison: is the Moon's disc, seen from the ground, wider than the Sun's? Right now it only just is, and only near perigee — which is why totality is rare, brief, and seen from a narrow band. Move the Moon out and it stops being possible at all; every solar eclipse becomes annular, a ring of Sun around a Moon too small to cover it.

The filled disc is the larger of each body's range, the outline the smaller. That the two ranges overlap at all is a coincidence of this moment in the Earth's history — the Moon is receding, and in a few hundred million years totality will have ended for good.
