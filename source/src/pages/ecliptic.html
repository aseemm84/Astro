The ecliptic drawn as a wheel — the twelve rāśis, the twenty-seven nakṣatras, and the nine grahas at their true longitudes — for any moment and any place, with no birth chart required. Give it a target moment and a span, and it will play the sky through that span so the motion itself is visible: the fast inner grahas sweeping, the slow outer ones barely stirring, and a retrograde loop closing on itself.

The wheel

The span, and playing through it

The span is how far either side of the target moment the wheel will travel. The scrubber is that whole span laid out end to end, so dragging it is dragging time; Play runs it from one end to the other and holds at the far end rather than looping, because a loop makes a return look like a motion.

What is drawn

How to read it

The wheel is sidereal and the longitudes are true. Each graha sits at its own degree within its rāśi, not at the sign’s centre, and the degree is printed beside it. A graha moving backwards against the stars is marked R; the Sun and Moon never are. Where two grahas would overlap they are moved onto separate rings rather than drawn on top of one another, so a conjunction reads as two discs close in angle rather than one disc hiding another.

The trails slider draws where each graha has been over the preceding part of the span, which is the only way a retrograde loop becomes visible as a loop: the trail doubles back on itself and crosses.

The tilt slider lays the whole wheel back out of the flat chart convention. At zero it is the conventional flat wheel; raised, the rings and the grahas lean together, and each graha also lifts by its own ecliptic latitude — so the Moon can be seen crossing the plane at a node rather than merely said to. Jyotiṣa works in longitude alone and treats every graha as sitting exactly on the ecliptic, which is why the flat wheel is the convention; the lift is a way of seeing what the numbers mean, not a classical quantity.

What the tilt is, exactly. It is an orthographic lean — the wheel as a circle seen from far off, which is an ellipse. It is not a perspective view: the near edge is not drawn larger than the far one. That distinction is worth stating because an earlier version of this control leaned only the grahas and left the rings circular, so every graha was drawn displaced from the rāśi it was actually in. The whole wheel now carries one transform, so a graha cannot leave its sign however far it is tilted.

The graha surfaces

Each graha is drawn as a lit sphere rather than a lettered disc: the surface is built pixel by pixel in your browser and lit from the direction of the Sun. Nothing is downloaded — there is no image file anywhere in this, and the same renderer draws the bodies in the Motion reels.

  • The Moon is drawn at its real phase, taken from its elongation from the Sun at the moment shown. Near a new Moon it is therefore almost entirely dark, which is the honest answer; the hairline at its edge is there so a dark Moon can still be found. Every other graha is drawn full, which is what a token on a chart wheel means.
  • Rāhu and Ketu have no surface and are given none. They are chāyā grahas — the two points where the Moon’s path crosses the ecliptic — not bodies, and inventing a photosphere for a node would be a picture of something that does not exist. They are drawn as shadow discs.
  • The sizes are a drawing convention, not a measurement. The Sun is drawn largest and Mercury smallest so that each reads as itself; this is neither apparent magnitude nor true angular diameter. The Sun and Moon each subtend about half a degree, and every other graha is a point.
  • Turn the surfaces off and the lettered discs come back, which is also what happens by itself if the surface renderer cannot load.

Palettes

The palette recolours the ground, the rings, the dividers and the lettering. It deliberately does not recolour the grahas: Maṅgala stays rust and Śukra stays pale whatever sits behind them, because those colours belong to the body rather than to the decoration. The one piece of chrome that carries hue of its own is the band of elements behind the rāśi names, so a palette adjusts its strength instead of replacing it — and Monochrome takes it away altogether.

Changing the varga redraws the same grahas at their divisional longitudes — a graha’s position within its varga sign, stretched across the thirty degrees, so motion within a division is visible rather than being snapped to the sign. Triṃśāṁśa is the exception: its five parts are unequal, so a graha there is placed at the centre of its varga sign.
