The ecliptic drawn as a wheel — the twelve rāśis, the twenty-seven nakṣatras, and the nine grahas at their true longitudes — for any moment and any place, with no birth chart required. Give it a target moment and a span, and it will play the sky through that span so the motion itself is visible: the fast inner grahas sweeping, the slow outer ones barely stirring, and a retrograde loop closing on itself.

The wheel

The span, and playing through it

The span is how far either side of the target moment the wheel will travel. The scrubber is that whole span laid out end to end, so dragging it is dragging time; Play runs it from one end to the other and holds at the far end rather than looping, because a loop makes a return look like a motion.

What is drawn

How to read it

The wheel is sidereal and the longitudes are true. Each graha sits at its own degree within its rāśi, not at the sign’s centre, and the degree is printed beside it. A graha moving backwards against the stars is marked R; the Sun and Moon never are. Where two grahas would overlap they are moved onto separate rings rather than drawn on top of one another, so a conjunction reads as two discs close in angle rather than one disc hiding another.

The trails slider draws where each graha has been over the preceding part of the span, which is the only way a retrograde loop becomes visible as a loop: the trail doubles back on itself and crosses.

Why there is no tilt here. This wheel is flat, which is the chart convention: Jyotiṣa works in longitude alone and treats every graha as though it sat exactly on the ecliptic. A tilt control was built for this page and then removed, because the renderer tilts the grahas without tilting the rings that are the ecliptic plane, so a tilted wheel drew each graha displaced from the rāśi it is actually in. A control that misplaces a graha is worse than no control. For the sky laid back into the plane you would really see it in — with each graha at its own ecliptic latitude, and the local horizon across it — use the Star wheel, which projects for that from the start.

Changing the varga redraws the same grahas at their divisional longitudes — a graha’s position within its varga sign, stretched across the thirty degrees, so motion within a division is visible rather than being snapped to the sign. Triṃśāṁśa is the exception: its five parts are unequal, so a graha there is placed at the centre of its varga sign.
