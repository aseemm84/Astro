The tithi either side of now

A tithi is not a day. It is the Moon pulling twelve degrees further from the Sun, and because the Moon’s speed varies by about fifteen per cent over a month, a tithi runs anywhere from about nineteen hours to about twenty-six. So it drifts against the clock: one can span two sunrises, and one can be skipped entirely. The strip shows the run of them around the present moment, with each band numbered within its pakṣa.

Divisions of the daylight

Later tradition. Horā, choghaḍiyā and rāhu kāla are not in Bṛhat Parāśara Horā Śāstra. They are widely used in practice and they are shown here for that reason, kept apart from the classical material above rather than mixed into it — the same separation this site keeps between verse, translator commentary and modern practice.

All three cut the span between sunrise and sunset — or sunrise to sunrise, for the horā — into equal parts and assign each part to a graha by weekday. Conventions differ: some traditions divide day and night separately into twelve unequal hours each, where this page uses the commoner twenty-four equal parts from sunrise. Where a published pañcāṅga differs from this page on rāhu kāla, the convention is usually the reason.

Why the day begins at sunrise

A civil day begins at midnight by agreement. A Hindu day begins at sunrise, and that is not a convention but the thing itself: the vāra turns there, and the tithi running at sunrise is the one that names the day, whatever happens later. An instant at two in the morning therefore belongs to the previous day’s reckoning, which is why a birth before sunrise carries the earlier weekday.

This is also why the place matters so much. Sunrise in Delhi and sunrise in Abu Dhabi are not the same moment, so the same instant can fall on different Hindu days in the two cities — and an observance fixed to a tithi at sunrise can fall on different civil dates. A pañcāṅga is only valid for the place it was computed for.
