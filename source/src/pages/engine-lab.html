This site carries more than one engine. A modern ephemeris computes every ordinary page; a working Sūrya Siddhānta computes the classical one; and a third tier runs the siddhānta’s own equations on constants measured against a modern sky. This page puts all three to the same questions and measures what their disagreements are actually worth — because an error in arcseconds means nothing until it is converted into the units the work is done in. No birth chart required, and everything below is computed in your browser as you read.

The four engines, in plain wordsचतुर्-यन्त्रम्

An engine here means a method of calculating where the planets are at a given moment. Every column on this page is headed by one of four, and they are always shown in the same order.

Words used on this page
Arcsecond (″), arcminute (′), degree (°)
Units of angle on the sky. 60 arcseconds make an arcminute and 60 arcminutes make a degree; the full circle is 360°. The Moon’s disc is about half a degree wide.
Rāśi, nakṣatra, pāda, navāṃśa
The ways the circle is divided: 12 signs of 30°, 27 lunar mansions of 13°20′, each mansion in 4 quarters (pādas) of 3°20′, and the navāṃśa, a ninth of a sign (also 3°20′).
Tithi, karaṇa, yoga, vāra
Parts of the Hindu day. A tithi is each 12° the Moon moves ahead of the Sun; a karaṇa is half a tithi; a yoga comes from the Sun and Moon’s longitudes added together; the vāra is the weekday.
Saṃkrānti
The moment the Sun enters a new sidereal sign — the start of a solar month.
Lagna
The sign and degree rising on the eastern horizon at a given moment and place.
Ayanāṃśa, frame
Where the zodiac is taken to start. Different conventions put the start a little differently; comparing two engines in two different frames would mostly measure the frames, so the page says which frame each column is in.
Measured, convention
“Measured” means checked against an independent calculation. “Convention” means a choice with no single correct value, such as which ayanāṃśa to use; a difference between two conventions is not an error.
Holdout
The moments kept back when the fitted engine was tuned, so its accuracy is judged on moments it never saw.

Ask all four the same questionचतुर्-प्रश्नः

Arcseconds are the wrong unit for judging an engine, because nobody reads a chart in arcseconds. What a reader wants to know is whether the answer changes — whether the nakṣatra is the same nakṣatra, whether the tithi is the same tithi. So pick the question rather than the body, and the last column says whether the four engines actually disagree about the answer.

How often they agree, over a span you choose

One moment proves nothing — the four engines might happen to agree today and part company tomorrow. So this samples the question across a span and reports the rate. Watch what happens to the tithi: the siddhānta names the same nakṣatra as the modern engine most of the time and the same vāra always, because a weekday is arithmetic on a day count and carries no position at all — but the tithi, which is the difference of two longitudes and so collects both errors, is the one a reader would notice.

What an arcsecond is worthकला-मूल्यम्

This is the conversion that makes the rest of the page meaningful, and it is the one most often skipped. An engine’s accuracy is quoted in arcseconds; a chart is read in rāśis, nakṣatras, pādas, aṁśas and daśā periods. Drag the slider and watch the same quantity restated in every one of those units at once. The markers underneath are where each engine and each body actually sits, measured.

How far each engine is from the real sky, and what that changes

The slider shows any error you like; this table shows the errors the three engines actually have, measured, and turns each into something a reader of a chart or an almanac would notice. “Typical” is the root-mean-square difference and “worst” the largest seen. All three are measured over 1800–2200: the modern engine against the professional reference, and the two Siddhānta engines against the modern sky, with the fitted figures taken on moments the fit never saw. Rāhu and Ketu are left out: the fit does not adjust them, so both Siddhānta engines place them as the text does.

The one that surprises people. Traversing one nakṣatra of 13°20′ traverses that nakṣatra lord’s whole mahādaśā — six years for the Sun, twenty for Venus — so the exchange rate between the Moon’s position and daśā time is lord-specific, not one fixed number. A full circuit of the nine lords is one hundred and twenty years and spans nine nakṣatras, which is one hundred and twenty degrees, giving a cycle average of exactly one year of daśā per degree of Moon. The rate is shown above, computed rather than quoted. It is still steep enough that an error far too small to move the Moon out of its pāda shifts a mahādaśā boundary by a day or two, and one minute of birth-time uncertainty is worth several days of daśā — a larger correction than any engine on this page contributes.

Correction. This note previously said the cycle mapped onto a single nakṣatra, and gave a fortnight per mahādaśā boundary and a month per minute of birth time. Those figures were too large by a factor of between six and twenty — the factor being 120 divided by the lord’s years.

The engines are not the problemसम्प्रदाय-भेदः

Everything above, and every convention this site has to choose between, on one logarithmic ruler from an arcsecond to ten degrees. The ruler is the argument: the gap between the best and worst engine is a matter of arcminutes, while the gap between two defensible conventions — which ayanāṁśa, which anchor star, which definition of sunrise — is measured in degrees. Anyone comparing this site’s output with another almanac will be looking at the right-hand end of this ruler, not the left.

Where the modern engine stops being certifiedसीमा

The modern engine is held to an independent computation of the sky over 1800–2200, and outside that window the honest statement is a disagreement rather than an error, because the reference degrades away from the present too. What it agrees with is the modern engine. That engine is held to an independent sky only over 1000–3000 CE; before and after, both are models, and ΔT — the Earth’s own clock — is uncertain by hours at the ends of the span. So read the residuals as how well this engine reproduces modern theory, not as how well either one knows the sky in 3000 BCE.
