Kālasetu Siddhānta — कालसेतु, “the bridge across time” — is this site’s fourth engine. It keeps the Sūrya Siddhānta’s own way of counting time and carries a modern geometry on it, and it is fitted across eight thousand six hundred years, from 5000 BCE to 3600 CE, in the Samanvaya–Ecliptic frame. Everything on this page is computed in your browser from the engine itself, as you read; no birth chart is needed.

What Kālasetu isकिम्

An engine here means a method of calculating where the grahas are at a given moment. The site already had three: the modern ephemeris, the Sūrya Siddhānta as written, and the Sūrya Siddhānta with re-measured constants. The last two are both pinned to the present — the text as written is degrees out everywhere, and the fitted tier was fitted on 1800–2200 and comes apart away from it. Kālasetu was built to hold across the whole of recorded and pre-recorded history the tradition speaks of, from before the Kali epoch to sixteen centuries from now.

Kept from the text

The count of time. Every mean place begins as the Later Sūrya Siddhānta’s own: its revolutions in a mahāyuga, times the days elapsed since the Kali epoch at Laṅkā sunrise (the ahargaṇa), over its 1,577,917,828 civil days. And the order of the work: mean place, then manda (the body’s own orbit), then śīghra (the Sun’s), then the karṇa, the true distance.

Corrected, in the tradition’s own way

Every fitted number is a correction on that count of the kind the tradition itself made: a bīja on a mean motion, a saṃskāra on an epoch, a re-measured mandocca (apogee). They are allowed to drift slowly, because over eight millennia apogees move and orbits change shape.

Not the text’s

The geometry. The orbits are solved exactly; the śīghra uses true distances (Nīlakaṇṭha’s revision, c. 1500, applied to all five planets); the Moon carries Candraśekhara Sāmanta’s evection, variation and annual equation and the further terms of the modern lunar series; Guru and Śani carry their great inequality; and the engine is handed the Earth’s clock, ΔT. None of this is presented as the Sūrya Siddhānta’s answer.

Because Kālasetu was fitted to the site’s modern engine, “how close” below always means how close to the modern engine. That engine is held to an independent computation of the sky only over 1000–3000 CE. Before and after, both are models.

    The Moon’s termsचन्द्र-संस्काराः

    The Moon is the graha a chart leans on hardest — nakṣatra, tithi and every Viṃśottarī date — and the one the Sūrya Siddhānta gets least right, because the text has a single correction to the Moon’s mean place where the Moon needs dozens.     Jupiter and Saturn across eight thousand yearsगुरु-शनि

    Five of Saturn’s years are very nearly two of Jupiter’s, so the two pull on each other in step for centuries at a time. The result is a slow swing in both their places with a period of about 880 years — the great inequality, close to a degree for Saturn at its largest. No Indian text has it, and without it no fixed set of constants can hold either planet across eight millennia. Kālasetu carries it, with its companions and Saturn’s terms with Uranus. What is drawn here is the proof that it works: Jupiter’s and Saturn’s difference from the modern engine, every 25 years across the whole span. An engine without the great inequality would show an 880-year wave of nearly a degree; this one stays within a few arcminutes.

    The Earth’s clockकाल-भेदः

    A siddhānta counts civil days, and civil days are not uniform: the Earth’s rotation slows, so a day in 5000 BCE was a fraction of a second shorter, and the fractions add up. The accumulated difference is called ΔT. It is the one input Kālasetu takes from outside itself, and it is the one thing no Indian text supplies. Without it the Moon was out by 1.5′; with it, about 0.6′.

    Rāhu and Ketuराहु-केतू

    The mean node moves backwards smoothly, about 19⅓° a year. The true node — where the Moon’s orbit actually crosses the ecliptic at that instant — wobbles about it by up to a degree and a half. The two Sūrya Siddhānta engines give the mean node only; Kālasetu gives both. Ketu is always exactly opposite Rāhu.     What its error costs a chartफल-मूल्यम्

    An error in arcminutes means nothing until it is turned into the units a chart is read in. This turns each graha’s typical held-back error into a share of a nakṣatra pāda (3°20′), a share of a ṣaṣṭyāṃśa (D60, 30′), and the time by which it would move the date that graha changes sign or nakṣatra.

    What it does not claimमर्यादा

    • It agrees with the modern engine, not with the sky. The modern engine is held to an independent reference only over 1000–3000 CE. Before and after, both are models.
    • A clock-time moment in the far past is not knowable to arcminutes by anyone. The Earth’s clock, ΔT, is measured from timed eclipses only back to 720 BCE. Before that it is extrapolated, and the two published estimates of its error disagree: at 5000 BCE, about ±1 hour on one and ±7 hours on the other. That is ±0.6° to ±4° of Moon and ±15° to ±100° of lagna for a birth time given on a clock. Kālasetu’s arcminutes are for a moment of uniform time; for a moment on a clock before about 1000 BCE, read the Moon’s pāda, the lagna and the finer divisions as uncertain whatever engine is used.
    • “Perfect” is not claimed. Mars and Saturn are the weakest — one to two arcminutes typical, and up to about twelve at close opposition or at the ends of the span.
    • It places grahas only. Sunrise, the lagna and eclipse searches stay with the modern engine, as they do for every Siddhānta engine on the site.
    • Outside 5000 BCE – 3600 CE it still answers, but nothing was tested there; a chart cast outside the span says so in capitals.
    • Its geometry is modern. It is the Sūrya Siddhānta’s count of time carrying a modern geometry, and it is never presented as the text’s answer. The text as written is on the Sūrya Siddhānta page.
    • Kundli cannot yet take a BCE date in its date field; a BCE moment can be explored here and in Engine Lab.
