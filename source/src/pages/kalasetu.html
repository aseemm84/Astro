Kālasetu Siddhānta — कालसेतु, “the bridge across time” — is this site’s fourth engine. It keeps the Sūrya Siddhānta’s own way of counting time and carries a modern geometry on it, and it is fitted across sixteen thousand six hundred years, from 13000 BCE to 3600 CE, in the Samanvaya–Ecliptic frame, against NASA JPL’s DE422 ephemeris and a numerical integration of the Solar System started from it. Every number carries its own error bar for its era. Everything on this page is computed in your browser from the engine itself, as you read; no birth chart is needed.

What Kālasetu isकिम्

An engine here means a method of calculating where the grahas are at a given moment. The site already had three: the modern ephemeris, the Sūrya Siddhānta as written, and the Sūrya Siddhānta with re-measured constants. The last two are both pinned to the present — the text as written is degrees out everywhere, and the fitted tier was fitted on 1800–2200 and comes apart away from it. Kālasetu was built to hold across the whole of recorded and pre-recorded history the tradition speaks of, from before the Kali epoch to sixteen centuries from now.

Kept from the text

The count of time. Every mean place begins as the Later Sūrya Siddhānta’s own: its revolutions in a mahāyuga, times the days elapsed since the Kali epoch at Laṅkā sunrise (the ahargaṇa), over its 1,577,917,828 civil days. And the order of the work: mean place, then manda (the body’s own orbit), then śīghra (the Sun’s), then the karṇa, the true distance.

Corrected, in the tradition’s own way

Every fitted number is a correction on that count of the kind the tradition itself made: a bīja on a mean motion, a saṃskāra on an epoch, a re-measured mandocca (apogee). They are allowed to drift slowly, because over eleven millennia apogees move and orbits change shape.

Not the text’s

The geometry. The orbits are solved exactly; the śīghra uses true distances (Nīlakaṇṭha’s revision, c. 1500, applied to all five planets); the Moon carries Candraśekhara Sāmanta’s evection, variation and annual equation and the further terms of the modern lunar series; Guru and Śani carry their great inequality; and the engine is handed the Earth’s clock, ΔT. None of this is presented as the Sūrya Siddhānta’s answer.

What “how close” means on this page. From 3000 BCE to 3000 CE it means how close to JPL DE422, NASA’s long-span ephemeris. Outside that, for the Sun and planets it means how close to a numerical integration of the Solar System started from DE422’s own positions; run across DE422’s own six thousand years, that integration never strays more than 9″ from it. For the Moon and Rāhu–Ketu before 3000 BCE there is no independent reference at all, and the page says so wherever it applies. Kālasetu was first fitted to the site’s modern engine; that engine’s own error before 1000 BCE turned out to be large, and is shown below beside Kālasetu’s.

The sky on any day in sixteen thousand yearsकाल-द्रष्टा

Choose a moment — BCE or CE, any day between 1 January 13000 BCE and 31 December 3600 CE — and Kālasetu places the nine grahas. Beside each one is the error measured for that graha in that era: its typical size, its bias (whether it runs early or late on average) and the worst case, from moments the fit never saw. The modern engine’s answer is in the last columns for comparison. Dates are in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.

The nine grahas are the same seen from anywhere on Earth (they are computed from the Earth’s centre). A place sets two things: the lagna, which depends on where the horizon is, and how a clock time there turns into Universal Time. Before standard time zones (the 1880s), a place kept local mean time, set by its own longitude — so for an ancient moment that is the natural choice.

How one longitude is madeगणित-क्रमः

Pick a graha and follow its place at the moment above from the day count to the answer. Every number in these steps is the engine’s own, recorded as it computes, so the explanation and the result cannot drift apart.

    Three fits, and what each era can be trusted toत्रि-संस्करणम्

    One set of numbers cannot hold sixteen millennia: a single fit stretched to 8000 BCE already cost the present-day Moon half an arcminute. So Kālasetu carries three fitted sets of the same engine, each with its own centre of time — Core (5000 BCE – 3600 CE), Early (9000 – 3000 BCE) and Deep (13000 – 8000 BCE). They overlap, and across the middle of each overlap the answer is blended from one to the next, so scrolling across a seam does not jump. Auto, the default everywhere on the site, reads each date from the fit made for it.

    The Moon’s termsचन्द्र-संस्काराः

    The Moon is the graha a chart leans on hardest — nakṣatra, tithi and every Viṃśottarī date — and the one the Sūrya Siddhānta gets least right, because the text has a single correction to the Moon’s mean place where the Moon needs dozens.     Jupiter and Saturn, and the great inequalityगुरु-शनि

    Five of Saturn’s years are very nearly two of Jupiter’s, so the two pull on each other in step for centuries at a time. The result is a slow swing in both their places with a period of about 880 years — the great inequality, close to a degree for Saturn at its largest. No Indian text has it, and without it no fixed set of constants can hold either planet across sixteen millennia. Kālasetu carries it, with its companions and Saturn’s terms with Uranus. What is drawn here is the proof that it works: Jupiter’s and Saturn’s difference from the modern engine every 10 years over 1000–3600 CE, where the modern engine is sound. An engine without the great inequality would show an 880-year wave of nearly a degree; this one stays within a few arcminutes. For the whole span, against DE422 and the integration, see the table above.

    The Earth’s clockकाल-भेदः

    A siddhānta counts civil days, and civil days are not uniform: the Earth’s rotation slows, so a day in 13000 BCE was a fraction of a second shorter, and the fractions add up. The accumulated difference is called ΔT. It is the one input Kālasetu takes from outside itself, and it is the one thing no Indian text supplies. The reference uses the same ΔT, with the tidal slowing of the Moon that ΔT’s tables assume.

    Rāhu and Ketuराहु-केतू

    The mean node moves backwards smoothly, about 19⅓° a year. The true node — where the Moon’s orbit actually crosses the ecliptic at that instant — wobbles about it by up to a degree and a half. The two Sūrya Siddhānta engines give the mean node only; Kālasetu gives both. Ketu is always exactly opposite Rāhu.     What its error costs a chartफल-मूल्यम्

    An error in arcminutes means nothing until it is turned into the units a chart is read in. This turns each graha’s typical held-back error into a share of a nakṣatra pāda (3°20′), a share of a ṣaṣṭyāṃśa (D60, 30′), and the time by which it would move the date that graha changes sign or nakṣatra.

    What it does not claimमर्यादा

    • It agrees with a reference, not with the sky directly. From 3000 BCE to 3000 CE the reference is JPL DE422. Outside it, the Sun and planets come from an integration started from DE422; it has no asteroids, and across DE422’s own span it stayed within 9″ (Mars) and 3″ (the rest). Over ten more millennia that error grows, but on any reasonable estimate it stays under an arcminute.
    • The Moon and Rāhu–Ketu before 3000 BCE are extrapolated, not measured. Kālasetu’s Moon is fitted to DE422 and carried back by its own secular terms. The only other long Moon to hand, the modern engine’s brought onto DE422’s tides, differs from it by about a degree at 5000 BCE and several by 13000 BCE. That spread is printed as the Moon’s error there, and it is a real limit: how fast the tides slowed the Moon in the Ice Age is not known to that precision.
    • A clock-time moment in the far past is not knowable to arcminutes by anyone. The Earth’s clock, ΔT, is measured from timed eclipses only back to 720 BCE. Before that it is extrapolated, and the two published estimates of its error disagree: at 5000 BCE, about ±1 hour on one and ±7 hours on the other; at 8000 BCE, about ±2 hours and ±18 hours; at 13000 BCE, about ±5 hours and ±2 days. At 5000 BCE that is ±0.6° to ±4° of Moon and ±15° to ±100° of lagna for a birth time given on a clock; from 8000 BCE the Moon’s nakṣatra is uncertain and the lagna is not knowable at all. Kālasetu’s arcminutes are for a moment of uniform time; for a moment on a clock before about 1000 BCE, read the Moon’s pāda, the lagna and the finer divisions as uncertain whatever engine is used.
    • “Perfect” is not claimed. The planets are about an arcminute or better typical in every era; Mars, at close opposition, reaches several. The table above gives every graha’s figure era by era.
    • The modern engine is not sound before about 1000 BCE. Its planets are a truncated series built for the present, and its Moon carries a different tidal slowing from the one ΔT assumes: against DE422 its Moon is half a degree out by 1000 BCE and a degree and a half by 3000 BCE, and before that its Saturn drifts by degrees. That is the site’s main engine, and it is reported here, not yet changed.
    • It places grahas only. Sunrise, the lagna and eclipse searches stay with the modern engine, as they do for every Siddhānta engine on the site.
    • Outside 13000 BCE – 3600 CE it still answers, but nothing was tested there; a chart cast outside the span says so in capitals.
    • Its geometry is modern. It is the Sūrya Siddhānta’s count of time carrying a modern geometry, and it is never presented as the text’s answer. The text as written is on the Sūrya Siddhānta page.
    • Kundli cannot yet take a BCE date in its date field; a BCE moment can be explored here and in Engine Lab.
