Each limb is a condition on a longitude, so each one ends at an instant rather than at midnight. The strip below is the day from sunrise to the next sunrise, with every segment that touches it and the moment each one closes.

The tithi, in detail

A tithi runs from about nineteen hours to about twenty-six, because the Moon’s speed varies by some fifteen per cent over a month. So it drifts against the clock: one can span two sunrises and be counted twice, and one can begin and end between two sunrises and be counted not at all. Both are named below when they happen.

How to read the wheel

Why it is tilted. A flat zodiac wheel draws the ecliptic as a circle around the observer, which is the one thing it is not. Seen from a place on the Earth the ecliptic is a great circle crossing the horizon at an angle that changes through the day, and the tilt here is what carries that: the horizon line stays put, the zodiac turns through it, and a graha below the line is a graha below the horizon — not visible, whatever the chart says about it.

The outer band is the twelve rāśis, the inner the twenty-seven nakṣatras. Each yogatārā — the junction star a nakṣatra is named for — is plotted at its own longitude and its own latitude, which is why they do not sit on the ecliptic line: Citrā is nearly two degrees south of it, and Revatí almost exactly on it. The grahas are plotted the same way, so the Moon visibly departs from the line and the Sun never does.
