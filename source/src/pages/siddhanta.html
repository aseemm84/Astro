Everything on this page is computed in your browser, twice — once the way the Sūrya Siddhānta does it and once with this site's modern ephemeris — so the two can be set beside each other.

What this is, and what it is not

Every other page on this site computes positions from a modern ephemeris. This one computes them the way the Sūrya Siddhānta does: from the number of revolutions each graha makes in a Mahāyuga, the number of civil days in that Mahāyuga, and a pair of epicycle corrections. It is not a better method and it is not offered as one.

It is here because the whole interpretive apparatus this site teaches was built on numbers produced this way. A student who has never seen what those numbers are cannot judge how much of a classical rule rests on astronomy and how much on arithmetic that has since been improved — and that judgement is worth more than either set of figures.

This is not BPHS. Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra is a work of horoscopy and supplies no planetary theory at all; the positions a classical reading was done from came from a siddhānta, and this is one of those. Nothing on this page is presented as BPHS doctrine.

No verse is cited here, deliberately. The parameters below are the standard Sūrya Siddhānta values as ordinarily implemented, and an implementation written from standard parameters is not the same thing as a reading of the text. This project's rule is that a citation is either exact or absent. A reader who wants the verses should take these numbers to the translation and find them there — which is a better use of the volumes than a reference printed here on anyone's say-so.

The parameters it uses

Generated from the engine itself, so what you read is what it is computing with. A manda or śīghra epicycle is given as two circumferences — one used at the ends of the odd quadrants of the anomaly and one at the even, with the value between them interpolated by the sine of the anomaly. That interpolation is part of the method.

The two skies

The working, step by step

The Sun and the Moon take one correction. The star-planets take four steps, and the order is the method rather than a refinement of it: half the śīghra, then half the manda, then the whole manda from what that gives, then the whole śīghra.

For Maṅgala, Guru and Śani the mean longitude is the graha's own and the śīghrocca is the Sun's mean place. For Budha and Śukra it is the other way about — the mean longitude is the Sun's, and the graha's own revolution count gives its śīghrocca. That inversion is how the system carries the fact that the inner planets go round the Sun, in a scheme that never says so.

Its own trigonometry

The Sūrya Siddhānta has no sine function. It has a table of twenty-four sines of a circle whose radius is 3438 — the number of minutes of arc in a radian, which is why that radius and not another — tabulated every 3°45′, interpolated linearly between entries, and read backwards through itself when an arc is wanted from a sine.

Computing the equations with a modern sine would give the answers a modern reader expects from the method and not the answers the method gives. So this engine computes with the table by default. You can switch it, and the difference is printed: it is smaller than the table's coarseness suggests, because the errors of the manda and śīghra steps are not in the same direction and partly cancel.

The day where you are standing

A siddhānta has to tell you when the Sun rises where you are, and that needs three things this engine now has: the declination of the Sun — taken of the sāyana longitude, because declination is measured from the equinox and not from the stars — the ascensional difference your latitude gives it, and the equation of time, which the text reaches in two named pieces rather than one.

The pañcāṅga, the old way

The five limbs follow from the two longitudes, so a Sūrya Siddhānta pañcāṅga is a Sūrya Siddhānta Sun and Moon put through the same arithmetic a modern one uses. The nakṣatra usually agrees with a modern almanac. The tithi often differs by one, and that is the Moon's accumulated drift arriving where a reader would actually notice it.

Eclipses

An eclipse is the hardest thing a classical astronomy is asked for, because it needs the Moon's latitude, the apparent sizes of three bodies and the instant of syzygy all at once — and an error in any of them turns a total eclipse into a partial one or into none.

The drift

The revolution counts imply a sidereal year, and that year is about three and a half minutes too long. Three and a half minutes is nothing; fifteen centuries of three and a half minutes is several days, and several days of the Sun is several degrees. This is that, drawn.

The Moon's line has a different cause and is worth looking at separately. Its sidereal month is barely a second too long — the synodic month is accurate to about a tenth of a second, which is remarkable — but a second a month for five thousand years is most of a day, and most of a day of the Moon is eleven degrees.

    Using it

    These positions are published into Studio Pro and Director Pro as ordinary nodes, so the older sky can be drawn by the same composers as the modern one and, more usefully, drawn alongside it. Look under siddhanta on the wall: the nine as the Sūrya Siddhānta places them, the two sets of positions on one ring, the gaps as bars, the working for any star-planet, and the drift as a series you can animate across whatever span you like.

    Nothing here is a prediction, and the difference between the two columns is not an error in either. It is the distance between a fifth-century theory and a modern one.
