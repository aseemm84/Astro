Everything on this page is computed in your browser, twice — once the way the Sūrya Siddhānta does it and once with this site's modern ephemeris — so the two can be set beside each other.

What this is, and what it is not

The rest of this site computes positions from a modern ephemeris unless you choose otherwise (Settings → Engine can make the Sūrya Siddhānta the engine for every page). This page always computes them both ways, side by side. The Siddhānta side computes them the way the Sūrya Siddhānta does: from the number of revolutions each graha makes in a Mahāyuga, the number of civil days in that Mahāyuga, and a pair of epicycle corrections. The two are set side by side and the page does not rank them: each is computed as its own method prescribes, and the differences between them are shown as differences.

It is here because the whole interpretive apparatus this site teaches was built on numbers produced this way. Seeing those numbers computed, beside the ones a modern ephemeris gives, shows exactly where the two agree and where they part — and where a classical rule depends on which of them it is read from.

This is not BPHS. Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra is a work of horoscopy and supplies no planetary theory at all; the positions a classical reading was done from came from a siddhānta, and this is one of those. Nothing on this page is presented as BPHS doctrine.

No verse is cited here, deliberately. The parameters below are the standard Sūrya Siddhānta values as ordinarily implemented, and an implementation written from standard parameters is not the same thing as a reading of the text. This project's rule is that a citation is either exact or absent. A reader who wants the verses should take these numbers to the translation and find them there — which is a better use of the volumes than a reference printed here on anyone's say-so.

Words used on this page
Mahāyuga
The great age of 4,320,000 years. The text gives how many times each planet goes round in one mahāyuga; dividing by the days in it gives the planet’s average daily motion.
Ahargaṇa
The count of days since the start of the Kali age (18 February 3102 BCE). Multiply it by a planet’s daily motion and you have its average position, the mean longitude.
Deferent and epicycle
The text corrects the mean position with small circles. A planet rides a small circle (the epicycle) whose centre moves round a large one (the deferent). The shift this produces, seen from the centre, is the equation added to or subtracted from the mean position.
Manda and śīghra
The two corrections. Manda (“slow”) accounts for the planet’s own uneven speed. Śīghra (“fast”) accounts, in modern terms, for watching from a moving Earth, and is what makes a planet appear to go backwards.
Mandocca, śīghrocca
The reference points for each correction: the manda apogee (where the planet is slowest) and the śīghra point that the second correction is measured from.
Anomaly
How far the planet is from its reference point; the size of each correction depends on it.
Paridhi
An epicycle’s circumference, given in degrees of the deferent.
Karṇa
The “hypotenuse”: the distance from the centre to the planet in the śīghra step.
Sine table, radius 3438
The text works with a table of 24 sines instead of a sine function. 3438 is the number of arcminutes in a radian, the radius the table is scaled to.
Sāyana, nirayana
Measured from the equinox (sāyana, the Western zodiac) or from the stars (nirayana, the Indian zodiac). The text works nirayana and converts when it needs to.
Ayanāṃśa libration
The text describes the zodiac’s starting point as swinging back and forth by up to 27° over 7,200 years, rather than moving steadily. That is the text’s own ayanāṃśa.
Declination, ascensional difference, equation of time
What is needed for sunrise: how far north or south the Sun is, how much that lengthens or shortens the day at your latitude, and the small correction between the Sun’s average and true timing.
Syzygy
A new or full moon: the moment the Sun and Moon line up. Eclipses can happen only then.
Bīja
“Seed”: a small correction to a revolution count, applied by later astronomers who compared the text with the sky.
Recension
A version of a text. The Sūrya Siddhānta survives in an older version (summarised by Varāhamihira) and a later one (the one usually translated, and the one this page computes).

The parameters it uses

Generated from the engine itself, so what you read is what it is computing with. A manda or śīghra epicycle is given as two circumferences — one used at the ends of the odd quadrants of the anomaly and one at the even, with the value between them interpolated by the sine of the anomaly. That interpolation is part of the method.

The two skies

The working, step by step

The Sun and the Moon take one correction. The star-planets take four steps, and the order is the method rather than a refinement of it: half the śīghra, then half the manda, then the whole manda from what that gives, then the whole śīghra.

For Maṅgala, Guru and Śani the mean longitude is the graha's own and the śīghrocca is the Sun's mean place. For Budha and Śukra it is the other way about — the mean longitude is the Sun's, and the graha's own revolution count gives its śīghrocca. That inversion is how the system carries the fact that the inner planets go round the Sun, in a scheme that never says so.

The geometry those four steps are doing

The words manda and śīghra name two circles, and no amount of prose substitutes for seeing them. Below is the construction the engine is actually evaluating: a deferent of radius 3438 carrying the mean graha, and an epicycle whose circumference the text gives in degrees of that deferent — so a parid hi of 14° means an epicycle 14/360 of the deferent’s own circumference. The displacement that epicycle produces, measured as an angle back at the centre, is the equation.

Two things are worth watching as you drag the anomaly. The epicycle breathes: this recension gives two circumferences, one for the ends of the odd quadrants and one for the even, and the value between them is interpolated by the sine of the anomaly — so the dashed rings are the two stated sizes and the solid one is what is being used at this instant. And for the śīghra step the karna — the hypotenuse from the centre to the graha — is not the radius, which is why that equation divides by it and the manda equation does not.

The breathing belongs to the later recension, and this page should not imply otherwise. The engine here implements the later Sūrya Siddhānta, the recension Burgess translated. The older Saura — the one Varāhamihira condensed in the Pañcasiddhāntikā, c. 505 CE — gives a single circumference per graha per epicycle and no interpolation at all: manda 70° for Maṅgala, 28° Budha, 32° Guru, 14° Śukra, 60° Śani; śīghra 234°, 132°, 72°, 260°, 40°. Set those against the pairs this engine uses and something striking appears — in six of the ten cases the older single value is exactly the even-quadrant member of the later pair, which reads as the later text having added a second circumference beside an inherited one. The apogee positions are identical in both. So what you are dragging below is a sixth- or seventh-century elaboration of a scheme that began simpler. Source: T. S. Kuppanna Sastry, tr., Pañcasiddhāntikā of Varāhamihira, ed. K. V. Sarma (PPST Foundation, Madras, 1993), Ch. XVII.1–3. Siddhāntic literature, not BPHS.

And the same two equations as curves across the whole anomaly, which is where the interpolation becomes visible: a pure epicycle would give a clean sine, and the kinks at the quadrant ends are the breathing above, drawn.

Its own trigonometry

The Sūrya Siddhānta has no sine function. It has a table of twenty-four sines of a circle whose radius is 3438 — the number of minutes of arc in a radian, which is why that radius and not another — tabulated every 3°45′, interpolated linearly between entries, and read backwards through itself when an arc is wanted from a sine.

Computing the equations with a modern sine would give the answers a modern reader expects from the method and not the answers the method gives. So this engine computes with the table by default. You can switch it, and the difference is printed: it is smaller than the table's coarseness suggests, because the table's departures from the modern sine in the manda and śīghra steps run in opposite directions and partly cancel.

Drawn, the table is almost indistinguishable from a true sine — which is both the point and the reason the residual has to be magnified before anything can be seen of it. The lower trace is that same difference multiplied fifty times. Its sawtooth is the signature of linear interpolation between tabulated points: near zero at every entry, largest in the middle of each interval, and mostly on one side of the line, because a sine is concave over each step and a straight line cuts the corner. Mostly, not always — the twenty-four entries are whole numbers, so each is itself rounded by up to seven tenths, and in a few intervals that rounding is enough to push the chord above the curve rather than below it. Two separate effects are therefore visible at once: the spacing of the grid, and the roundness of the numbers on it.

The day where you are standing

A siddhānta has to tell you when the Sun rises where you are, and that needs three things this engine now has: the declination of the Sun — taken of the sāyana longitude, because declination is measured from the equinox and not from the stars — the ascensional difference your latitude gives it, and the equation of time, which the text reaches in two named pieces rather than one.

The pañcāṅga, the old way

The five limbs follow from the two longitudes, so a Sūrya Siddhānta pañcāṅga is a Sūrya Siddhānta Sun and Moon put through the same arithmetic a modern one uses. The nakṣatra usually agrees with a modern almanac. The tithi often differs by one: that is where the difference between the two Moons first shows in something a reader of an almanac would notice.

Eclipses

An eclipse is the hardest thing a classical astronomy is asked for, because it needs the Moon's latitude, the apparent sizes of three bodies and the instant of syzygy all at once — so a small difference in any of them between two astronomies can make one find a total eclipse where the other finds a partial one or none.

The drift

The revolution counts imply a sidereal year about three and a half minutes longer than the modern one. Over fifteen centuries three and a half minutes a year accumulate to several days, and several days of the Sun is several degrees. This is that difference, drawn.

The Moon’s line has a different cause and is worth looking at separately. Its sidereal month is about a second longer than the modern value, and its synodic month within about a tenth of a second of it; a second a month for five thousand years is most of a day, and most of a day of the Moon is eleven degrees.

A correction, recorded rather than quietly folded in. Until November 2026 the Moon’s line on this chart was about twice as far from zero as it should have been, and the cause was not the text. This engine’s table of epoch longitudes carried the Moon’s node at 180° and omitted the Moon’s apsidal line altogether, leaving it at zero where the tradition puts it at 90° — which threw the Moon’s whole manda equation, some five degrees in amplitude, a quarter turn out of phase for every date computed. Supplying the constant drops the bare text’s lunar difference from the modern Moon from about 378′ to 221′, and lifts the agreement of a Sūrya Siddhānta pañcāṅga with a modern one from 62% to 90% on the nakṣatra and from 64% to 91% on the exact tithi. What remains is a difference between the two theories themselves: two lunar terms the modern one contains and this one does not, identified further down this page. The value is from Kuppanna Sastry’s notes to Pañcasiddhāntikā Ch. XVI, which give it for the later Sūrya Siddhānta as well as for Varāhamihira’s Saura; and it is confirmed without the text, by fitting the bare Moon against a modern sky across the whole circle, which lands between 80° and 86° depending on the sample.

Adjust the constants yourselfबीज-संस्कारः

The difference above is a few degrees. The tradition has its own practice for bringing a siddhānta into agreement with observation: bīja — a “seed”, a small adjustment to a revolution count applied by a later astronomer who had compared it with the sky. Adjusting constants this way is not a modern liberty taken with the text; it is what the siddhāntic tradition itself did for a thousand years.

So the knobs below are the ones the tradition itself treats as correctable, and nothing else. No equation changes and no term is added. Every slider moves a constant: revolutions in a Mahāyuga, the two circumferences of each epicycle, the direction of the apsis, the longitude at epoch. The figure beside each graha is its difference from this site’s modern engine, recomputed as you drag.

Varāhamihira’s Saura is a third set of constants rather than a third method: the Pañcasiddhāntikā’s own numbers for the same equations, from a recension older than the Sūrya Siddhānta this page otherwise computes. Of the five siddhāntas Varāhamihira condenses, the Saura alone uses epicycles, so it is the only one with a counterpart here at all.

Three things it shows. Its yuga is 1,577,917,800 civil days against 1,577,917,828 — the 28-day Ardharātrika gap, an implied year of 365.258750 d against 365.258756 d, which is half a second. Its epicycles do not breathe: the older text gives one circumference per body where this engine carries a pair, and six of its ten star-planet figures are exactly the engine’s even-quadrant member — so the interpolation that makes the epicycle swell and shrink is a later-recension feature rather than simply how the method works. And the mandocca agree exactly on all five star-planets, which is a result and not a coincidence.

What moves, against the text as written: Guru barely at all, because its revolutions are the one figure that agrees exactly; the Sun a few arcminutes, which is the civil-day gap and nothing else; and Budha by about 14° by now, growing with distance from the epoch, which is what a sixty-revolution difference on a śīghrocca has to do.

Some quantities in it are not Varāhamihira’s, and the tier names them rather than quietly absorbing them. The edition in hand is page images but for one appendix, and the OCR never reached the chapter carrying them; only their epoch longitudes are text-verified, and those are already what this engine ships.
